# Funnel Frameworks

Used by `SKILL.md`. These are the working methods behind every funnel, written for any industry. Apply them to the member's own offer and words. Never copy example wording from here into member assets, and never name where an idea came from.

## 1. Principles that apply to everything
1. **Sell what changes, not what is included.** People pay for a better situation. Lessons, calls, and steps are evidence, not the headline.
2. **Be concrete.** A promise like "grow your business" paints no picture. A small result someone can picture this month does.
3. **Give a taste, keep the meal.** Free material should hand over one real win and show what comes next. If the free piece solves everything, there is nothing left to buy.
4. **Move people, don't just inform them.** Good content changes a belief and gives a reason to act now.
5. **Name what failed.** Everyone has already tried something that did not work. Naming it removes their embarrassment and shows the offer is different. The thing that failed is an outdated method or process, never a person, a competitor, or a group.
6. **Let the first screen close the sale for the ready person.** Everything below it serves the person who needs more reassurance.
7. **Earn trust through detail and candor.** Use actual figures, named people (with their consent), honest limits, and terms stated plainly.

## 2. The result ladder
Define four steps for each kind of buyer.

| Step | When | How to test it |
|---|---|---|
| First win | Within hours to a week | Could they show someone a screenshot or a finished thing? |
| Small result | Within a week to a month | Could a stranger picture it? |
| Milestone | Within one to three months | Could they measure it? |
| Big change | Six to twelve months | Would they tell a friend about it? |

Lead with the small result. Use the first win to lower the risk of starting. Keep the milestone and big change for deeper on the page. In industries with outcome limits (see `compliance-by-industry.md`), the result is described as what the member does or provides, never a promised outcome.

### When results differ from person to person
1. Write one line per kind of buyer ("If you are in this situation, this means...").
2. Promise what the process produces, not what the person earns or gains. Something like "every inquiry gets a reply within the hour" is true for everyone.
3. Use ranges and real examples, never guarantees.
4. Describe an ordinary day after the fix: what stops, what happens by itself, what they see.

## 3. Listening to the customer
Before writing headlines, gather real phrases from the buyer:
- Reviews of the member's business and of nearby alternatives (especially the middling ones, which say what is missing)
- Questions and objections the member hears on calls and messages (the Objection Bank, if the member has one)
- Public discussions where the buyer asks for help, read from public pages only
- Anything the member pastes: messages, survey answers, call notes (never reuse private details or names)

Pull out four things: how they describe the pain, how they describe what they want, what they already tried, and what makes them hesitate. Use their wording in hooks, headlines, and bullets.

## 4. Checking and shaping the offer
### The four levers
How good the offer feels depends on four things: how much they want the result, how likely it seems to work, how long until the first benefit, and how much work it takes. For each weak spot, decide whether to change the offer, add a bonus, or fix the wording.
- Want: is it the result they actually named?
- Likelihood: proof, specifics, a clear plan, done-for-them pieces, honest terms.
- Speed: can the first benefit arrive sooner?
- Effort: what can be removed or done for them?

### Obstacles into pieces
1. List every obstacle between where they are and the result, before, during, and after they buy.
2. Turn each into a named piece (a template, a checklist, a short training, a done-for-you step, support).
3. Drop pieces that cost a lot to deliver and add little.
4. Group what is left by the result it creates, not by file type.

### Bonuses
Each bonus must remove one specific worry or speed up one specific result. Name it by what it does for them. Only state a value if it can be defended.

### Naming
Pattern: the result, plus a word that signals what kind of thing it is. A "plan" is simple to follow. A "system" runs predictably. A "playbook" repeats across situations. A "kit" holds ready-to-use pieces.

## 5. The one core message
### The statement
Write: **[Name of the thing]: [small visible result] without [the specific method they are tired of]**.
- The result is small, visible, reachable in a week to a month, and in the buyer's words.
- The "without" names an exact approach they have tried, never "without wasting time". Up to three.
Write ten versions, choose three, and say why.

### The quick clarity test (apply it to each statement, headline, and hook)
1. Could a stranger form a picture of the precise result?
2. Is it plain how this differs from approaches they have already used?
3. Would the right buyer think "that describes me"?
4. Can it realistically happen within a week to a month?
5. Does it come across as a modest, welcome win?
Rewrite until all five pass, and show the five answers.

### Supporting pieces
- **The old way:** the outdated method that let them down.
- **The belief shift:** "You do not need more of X. You need Y."
- **How it works:** one line on why this works when the old way did not.
- **The guide line:** two sentences that show you understand the problem and have helped others past it.
- **The cost of waiting:** what it costs to stay the same, stated factually with no scare tactics.

### Versions for every place
From the final statement, write a short bio line, five hooks, five email subject lines, three ad headlines, a page headline and subheadline, a one-sentence spoken pitch, and a comment-keyword call to action for social posts.

## 6. How ready the buyer is decides the page
| Where they are | Open with | Length |
|---|---|---|
| Ready to buy, knows the offer | The offer, the price, and the date | Very short |
| Comparing options | What makes yours different, plus proof | Short to medium |
| Knows the result they want, not your way | How it works and the "without" | Medium |
| Aware of the problem | Describe the problem using their wording, then reframe it | Medium to long |
| Notices only symptoms | A short story or unexpected fact about that symptom, or free educational content first | Long |

Cold ads usually reach people who know the problem or know the result they want. Email lists and past customers are usually ready or comparing. Match the page to the traffic, and use separate pages for different temperatures.

## 7. Pages
### Main offer page: decide first, prove below
Write the first screen for the visitor who is ready to act. Everything beneath it serves the visitor who wants more reassurance. Remove any section that does not settle a genuine question or concern.
1. **First screen.** A line naming who it is for, a headline with the small result and the "without", a subhead with the way it works or the biggest worry removed, three bullets on what changes, one piece of proof, and a button in the buyer's own words.
2. **The problem in their words.** Symptoms and what they tried. End with "it is not you, it is the old way".
3. **The shift.** The belief change and how it works.
4. **What changes.** The result ladder with timeframes, one line per kind of buyer if needed.
5. **Proof.** Concrete results with their setting: who got them, what was done, and over what period. Prefer real images to long paragraphs.
6. **What they get.** Each piece grouped by the result it creates.
7. **The offer.** Price, what is included, the chosen offer path, and any real deadline. Button.
8. **The guide.** A brief story: what you used to believe, what changed your mind, the better approach, what happened next, and your reason for creating this. It is not a resume.
9. **Who it is for and not for.** Three to five of each.
10. **Terms and any guarantee.**
11. **Questions.** One per real worry: time, skill, tech, "will it work for me", access, cost, support.
12. **Final button.** Repeat the result and the cost of waiting, in the same words as the first button.
Length follows the buyer-readiness table. Short paragraphs, one idea per section, headings a skimmer can follow, the button repeated after proof and after the offer, and mobile first.

### Sign-up page for a free resource
A headline with the quick win and the time it takes, three bullets on what they will be able to do, an image of the resource, a short form (name and email, and a phone number only when texting consent is documented, with a consent checkbox), and a button that states the result. No navigation. It should fit on one phone screen.

### Checkout (for products and programs)
Repeat the name and small result, list what is included in three to five lines, show one short testimonial (with permission), show the single optional add-on if there is one, state the terms plainly, and remove distractions.

### Thank-you page
Confirm what they just did, show how to reach a first win within ten minutes, state what will arrive and when, and suggest one fitting next step. The instructions for that first win matter more than anything else on the page.

### Membership page
Lead with the monthly result, show what a month looks like, share member wins, list what is included, frame the monthly price against the cost of the problem, and state how to cancel.

## 8. Hooks and ads
### Hook families by buyer readiness
- **Problem-aware (expose why the usual fix is not working):** "Why [problem] keeps happening even when you [common fix]", "The real reason [symptom] shows up for [buyer]", "Stop [thing they think they need] if you want [result]".
- **Result-aware (a surprising path):** "[Result] in [time] without [thing they assume is required]", "The less you [common tactic], the more [result]", "Three things I skipped to get [result]".
- **Comparing (show what is different):** "What most [category] won't tell you about [topic]", "Here is what changed when [buyer type] stopped [common habit]", "A few myths that keep [buyer] from [result]".
- **Skeptical or unaware (draw them in with a story):** a moment of doubt, a belief that turned out wrong, a small test that went badly, a client comment that changed how you work.
Every hook should challenge something the buyer assumed. If it creates no recognition, relief, or curiosity, it is not finished.

### Ad structure
For each ad set, write:
- **Angle:** pain, desire, the old way, proof, or story
- **Hook:** the first line or first three seconds
- **Body:** symptom, then the shift, then the small result, then proof, then the button (about 125 words or fewer for a feed ad, longer only for a story angle)
- **Headline** (about 40 characters or fewer) and a **description**
- **Creative direction:** talking to camera, a screen recording, a carousel, a before and after of a process (not a person's body or health), a testimonial image (with permission)
- **Message match:** the page should repeat the promise made in the ad, in similar words
Write three to five angles with two or three hooks each, so there is something to test. With a small budget, test hooks as ordinary posts first, then put money behind what already works.

### Calls to action
- **Social comment keyword:** "Comment [WORD] and I will send you [specific thing]."
- **Email or page:** name the result and the reason to act now, for example a shortcut to a result without the thing they dislike, or the step-by-step way to a result even if a common worry applies.
Urgency must be real (a true date, a true limit). Never invent scarcity.

## 9. Email follow-up
**After a free resource (five to seven emails)**
1. Deliver it, with how to get the first win.
2. A short story about why this matters.
3. The next problem the free resource reveals.
4. How it works, and the offer introduced.
5. Proof or a short case story.
6. Worries and questions answered.
7. A real deadline or a direct ask.

**For an offer or event (five to ten emails):** rotate teaching, surprising-path, comparison, and story emails. Put a soft mention at the end of teaching emails. Get more direct as the date nears, with two or three short emails in the last day.

**After a purchase (three to five emails):** welcome and first win, a check-in with the common snag, a success story, then the natural next step if one fits.

**For members:** a first-week win, a monthly "what's new", member highlights, and a note for people who went quiet.

Subject lines are short and lean on curiosity or the result. Write three per email.

## 10. Offer brief fields
Brand and voice; who buys and in what situation (list each kind of buyer); the offer (name, type, price, how it is delivered, how soon the first benefit); the buyer's situation now; what they already tried; proof available (and which is public); where the traffic comes from; the offer path and guarantee preference; what to build and for which platform; and the compliance categories that apply.

## 11. Building an industry adapter
When the member's industry has no adapter, build one with them and save it. It has six parts: the kinds of buyers and what each wants; the ten pieces of outdated advice this audience has been given; fifteen small, visible wins specific to the business; the words they use and the jargon to avoid; the trust signals that matter (licenses, numbers, years, affiliations); and the legal and advertising-platform rules that govern the industry.
